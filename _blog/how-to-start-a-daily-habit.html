A first-week plan you can actually try

Start with an action you can describe clearly: “After breakfast, I will read one page.” Choose a book you already want to read, leave it where you eat, and count the page as a complete session. Reading more is optional. This is a planning example, not a promise that reading will feel automatic in seven days.

Worked example: one page after breakfast
DayWhat happenedWhat to do next
MondayRead one page after breakfast.Leave the book beside the bowl for tomorrow.
TuesdayRead three pages.Keep the requirement at one page.
WednesdayRushed out and forgot.Record the miss honestly. Put the book back in view.
ThursdayRead one page.Continue the same plan; no catch-up session needed.
FridayRead one page after a late breakfast.Notice that the breakfast cue still worked.
SaturdayAte out and missed the cue.Try a weekend cue: after the first cup of tea at home.
SundayRead one page after tea.Review five completed sessions and two missed opportunities.

The useful result is learning where reading fits. Five sessions do not prove that a habit has formed, and two misses do not prove that the plan has failed. You now have a specific adjustment to test next week.

Step 1: Choose one habit to work on first

Pick something that matters to you and fits your current circumstances. Starting with one new action is our practical recommendation for keeping the experiment manageable, not a scientifically established limit on how many habits anyone can build. Keep existing routines that already work.

Write the behavior rather than only the outcome. “Become a reader” is a direction; “read one page after breakfast” is an action you can recognize and record. If you are undecided, browse these daily habit ideas and choose the easiest useful starting point.

Step 2: Define a minimum that counts

Set a version you can reasonably do on a busy day. One sentence in a journal, one page of a book, or a short walk can be a starting point. A two-minute limit is a useful design exercise, not a biological threshold. The minimum should still mean something to you.

  • Reading: one page, with more pages optional.
  • Journaling: one sentence about today.
  • Tidying: put away the items on one small surface.

Decide what “done” means before starting. If your minimum is one page, reading one page counts. Do not quietly raise the target every time you have an energetic day.

Step 3: Attach the action to a reliable cue

Use this sentence: “After I [something that already happens], I will [my minimum action].” Check that the first event happens on the days you intend to practice and that the materials are available there. A cue tied to your commute will need a different plan on days you work from home.

Repeating a behavior in a consistent context is supported by Lally and colleagues’ habit-formation study. It does not make any particular cue work for everyone. Choose a cue you can observe, then see whether you remember the action when it occurs.

Step 4: Use a reminder where it helps

A reminder can prompt the action while the routine is unfamiliar. Set it near the moment you can act, rather than at a time that sounds ideal on paper. If you keep dismissing it, move the reminder, change the cue, or reduce the task.

A visible object can help too: the open notebook on your desk or the book beside your breakfast place. You do not need several notifications for one simple action.

Step 5: Record what actually happened

Use a paper grid or an app to record completion against the schedule you chose. A check mark means the action happened, not that you intended to do it. Leave a missed session uncompleted. If possible, note the reason briefly: “away from home” is more useful at review time than “bad day.”

Step 6: Review and adjust after a miss

At the next reasonable opportunity, return to the minimum version. Avoid doubling the task as a penalty. If the same obstacle repeats, change one part of the plan: the cue, the size, the location, or the schedule.

“Never miss twice” can be a personal reminder to return, but it is not a research-backed deadline. Illness, travel, and other responsibilities may require a longer pause. Our guide to staying consistent explains how to diagnose recurring obstacles.

What to check if starting still feels difficult

Ask whether the action is useful to you now, whether the cue really occurs, and whether the minimum fits the time available. A plan can be poorly matched to your life without anything being wrong with you. You can simplify it, change it, or decide this is not the right habit to pursue.

Copy this plan: After ___, I will ___. My minimum is ___. I will keep ___ ready. If I miss, my next opportunity is ___. I will review the plan on ___.

Evidence and practical frameworks

This guide combines research on consistent context with practical planning examples. The seven-day plan and one-habit starting recommendation are editorial suggestions, not experimentally validated programs.

  • Lally et al. (2010), How are habits formed? Research on repetition and automaticity.
