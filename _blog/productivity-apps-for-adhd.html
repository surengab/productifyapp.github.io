If you have ADHD, you've probably been here. You find a new app, spend an evening setting it up beautifully, use it every day for nine days and then never open it again. The app didn't stop working. The novelty did.

So this isn't a ranking. It's a look at what tends to help, which app fits which problem, and how to set one up so it's still there in week five.

Before we start: we make Productify, one of the apps below, and we're an app company, not clinicians. Nothing here is medical advice, and no app replaces support from a qualified professional.

What makes a productivity app work for ADHD?

Most productivity apps assume you'll remember to open them, know how long things take, and start a task once it's written down. With ADHD, those are often the hard parts. A good app doesn't ask you to try harder. It removes steps.

  • It reminds you at the right moment. A nudge for each thing when it's relevant, not one morning list of everything.
  • It makes starting smaller. Breaking a task into first steps, or starting a timer, lowers the bar to begin.
  • It makes time visible. Timelines and countdowns help when "twenty minutes" and "two hours" feel about the same.
  • It's fast. If logging something takes more than a few seconds, it won't get logged.

Color themes, integrations and dashboards are nice. They're not why an app sticks.

Do productivity apps actually help with ADHD?

The evidence is thinner than app store pages suggest. Organizing and planning skills, such as keeping a calendar and task list and breaking overwhelming tasks into steps, are part of cognitive behavioral therapy for adult ADHD, which outperformed a relaxation-based comparison in a 2010 randomized trial. That trial tested therapy, though, not apps. A 2020 review of 109 ADHD apps found that none offered information on evidence for their effectiveness, and a 2025 review of reviews concluded that the effectiveness of digital ADHD tools, from apps to games, remains inconclusive because the evidence is low quality.

Our take: an app is a small supporting tool. It can help with specific friction, like forgetting or not starting. It won't fix everything, and if it isn't helping after a few weeks, that's information about the app, not about you.

Task app or habit app?

Most ADHD app lists mix two kinds of tool. A task app helps you remember one-off things: the dentist on Thursday, the form due Friday. A habit app helps you keep doing repeated things: medication, exercise, a daily focus block.

If your problem is forgetting one-off things, start with a task app. If your routines never quite stick, start with a habit app. If it's both, use one simple app of each. Two light apps you actually open beat one all-in-one system you abandon. Our habit tracker vs to-do list comparison covers the difference in more detail.

The best productivity apps for ADHD, by problem

There isn't one best app. There's the best app for the thing that keeps going wrong for you. Platforms and free plans below were checked in November 2026; confirm current prices in each store before you commit.

If you forget things the moment you think of them

Todoist (iPhone, Android, web, desktop) is a task app built around fast capture. Type "call the doctor tomorrow at 10" and it understands the date. Good for catching ideas before they disappear. The risk: a list that grows forever.

TickTick (iPhone, Android, web, desktop) combines tasks, a calendar, simple habits and a Pomodoro timer, and the free plan includes the timer and habits. Handy if you'd rather not juggle several apps, though all-in-one also means more settings to get lost in.

If you can't tell where the time goes

Tiimo (iPhone, iPad, Apple Watch, Android, web) shows your day as a visual timeline with icons and timers. It's built with neurodivergent users in mind, has a free basic plan, and won iPhone App of the Year at the 2025 App Store Awards.

If you can't get started

Goblin Tools (web, iPhone, Android) uses AI to break a task you're avoiding into small, concrete steps. Type "clean the kitchen" and you get a list you can actually begin. The website is free and the phone apps cost a small one-time price. A good companion to any other app.

Focusmate (web) pairs you with another person on video for a set work session, a form of body doubling. Many people with ADHD find it easier to start when someone else is quietly working too. The free plan covers three sessions a week. If a whole session on camera feels like too much, our look at accountability partners covers lighter ways to share progress.

If your phone keeps winning

Forest (iPhone, Android, Apple Watch, Chrome) grows a virtual tree while you stay off your phone. Leave the app and the tree dies. Slightly silly, surprisingly effective for some people. Its pricing has changed recently, so check the store before you buy.

If you want everything in one place

Notion (iPhone, Android, web, desktop) can hold tasks, notes, projects and habit trackers in one workspace. It's hugely flexible, and that's the danger: building the perfect Notion system can become a very satisfying way to avoid the actual work.

If routines never stick

Habitica (iPhone, Android, web) turns habits into a role-playing game, with points, levels and a party. Great if game rewards motivate you. Less great if losing health for a missed day makes you want to quit.

Habitify (iPhone, Android, Mac, web) is a clean habit tracker with reminders and progress stats. A solid choice if you're on Android as well as iPhone. Our habit tracker app comparison covers more options.

Productify (iPhone, Apple Watch). Disclosure: this is our app. It's a general habit tracker, not one designed specifically for ADHD. Each habit gets its own reminder, a check-in takes seconds from the app, a widget or your watch, and a missed day ends the current streak without erasing your history. Habit Duo lets you share one habit with one person and is part of Pro. The free plan covers up to 5 habits with no account, and Pro is $4.99 a month or $19.99 a year in the US. There's no Android version, and it won't manage tasks or a calendar, so pair it with a task app if you need that. For setting one up, see our guide to habit tracking with ADHD.

Which should you actually pick?

Pick the one problem that costs you most each week, and choose one app for that problem only.

  • Forgetting things: Todoist or TickTick.
  • Losing track of time: Tiimo.
  • Can't start: Goblin Tools or Focusmate.
  • Phone: Forest.
  • Routines: a simple habit tracker, such as Productify on iPhone or Habitify if you need Android too.

Then give it four weeks before you add anything else.

Why most ADHD apps get abandoned, and how to avoid it

The app matters less than the first two weeks. Most apps aren't dropped because they're bad. They're dropped when the novelty runs out, usually somewhere in week two.

Set it up in ten minutes, not one evening

The beautiful three-hour setup is the classic trap. It feels productive, burns the novelty in one go, and leaves nothing for day four. Add the bare minimum and use it today. Watch for the urge to customize colors, tags and categories before you've used the app once.

One problem, one app

Downloading five apps to "see which one works" turns the trial into a new project. Start with one app and the smallest useful setup: one list, or one habit. You can add more once the first one runs on its own.

Expect the week-two wobble

At some point you'll catch yourself looking at a shinier app. It's usually week two, and it's usually the novelty talking. Decide in advance that a missed day means picking it back up tomorrow, not that the app has failed. If you're tracking habits, our ADHD habit tracker guide covers tying reminders to real moments and keeping a bad-day version small, and how to start a daily habit walks through a first week.

Give it a four-week trial with an end date

Four weeks is long enough to get past the novelty and short enough to start. At the end, decide: keep it, simplify it, or swap it. Judge it on ordinary weeks rather than perfect ones. Did you open it on the days you didn't feel like it?

What to avoid

  • Spending your best focus hours setting up the system instead of using it.
  • Turning on every notification. Too many reminders become background noise.
  • Apps that punish missed days so harshly that you stop opening them.
  • Treating an app as a replacement for medical or professional support.
  • Blaming yourself when an app doesn't fit. It's a tool, not a test.

The best productivity app for ADHD isn't the one with the most features. It's the one you're still opening in week five. Pick one problem, pick one app, and start today rather than researching apps for another month.
