Stoic quotes are everywhere, but the Stoics themselves cared less about memorable lines than about training. Their letters and notebooks describe exercises to repeat: a few minutes of preparation in the morning, a review at night, a deliberate taste of hardship, a count of good days. Read with a habit tracker in mind, much of Stoic practice looks like a routine with a cue, an action, and a record.

This article turns eight of those exercises into small daily habits. For each one you get the original source, a version that takes a few minutes, and a simple way to track it. You don’t need to adopt the whole philosophy or practice all eight. One practice done on most days is a better start than a full Stoic regimen abandoned within a week.

What Stoic practice is, and what it isn’t

Stoicism began in Athens around 300 BCE with Zeno of Citium, who taught at the Stoa Poikile, the “painted porch” that gave the school its name. The writers most people read today are three Romans: Seneca (c. 4 BCE to 65 CE), a statesman and playwright; Epictetus (c. 50 to 135 CE), a teacher who had been enslaved in his youth; and Marcus Aurelius (121 to 180 CE), the emperor whose private notebook we now call the Meditations.

All three treated philosophy as something you do every day. Epictetus put the idea in terms any habit tracker would recognize:

“Every habit and faculty is maintained and increased by the corresponding actions: the habit of walking by walking, the habit of running by running.”

Epictetus, Discourses 2.18 (trans. George Long)

One common misunderstanding is worth clearing up first. In everyday English, “stoic” means keeping a stiff upper lip and not showing your feelings. That is not what these practices ask of you. In a 2022 study of 1,307 university students in New Zealand, Norway, and the United States, Karl and colleagues found that this everyday “stoic ideology,” especially the wish not to express or feel emotions, was associated with lower well-being. The study was correlational and measured the popular attitude rather than the ancient philosophy. The Stoics did want freedom from destructive passions, but their method was to examine the judgments behind an emotion, not to bottle it up.

1. The morning preview

“Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial.”

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.1 (trans. George Long)

Marcus opens Book 2 by rehearsing the day’s likely frustrations before they happen. He goes on to remind himself that these people act out of ignorance of what is good, and that he was made to work with them, not against them. Modern writers often call this kind of rehearsal premeditatio malorum, the advance consideration of troubles.

The aim is not to expect the worst from everyone. It is to choose your response while you are calm, rather than improvising one in the middle of a frustrating meeting.

Small daily version: Before you open email or messages, take two minutes. Name one situation today that could test you, such as a tense meeting, a long commute, or a tired evening at home, and decide on one response you want to practice. For mornings when getting up is the hard part, Marcus offers a second line: “I am rising to the work of a human being” (Meditations 5.1).

How to track it: A daily yes-or-no habit with a reminder at the time you usually wake. It fits easily into an existing morning routine: do it right after your first coffee, or before you unlock your phone.

2. The control check

“Some things are in our control and others not.”

Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 (trans. Elizabeth Carter)

This is the first line of the Enchiridion, a short handbook of Epictetus’ teaching compiled by his student Arrian. Epictetus counts our opinions, pursuits, desires, and aversions as in our control: in a word, our own actions. Our bodies, property, reputation, and position are not fully up to us.

Modern readers often call this the dichotomy of control. Applied to a worry, it splits one vague problem into two parts: what you can act on today, and what you can only accept or influence. A worry about a job interview, for example, contains a part that belongs to you (preparing three examples, sleeping well, leaving early) and a part that doesn’t (who else applied, the interviewer’s mood).

Small daily version: Once a day, write down the thing occupying your mind most. Underline the part you control and write one action for it. Set the rest aside for now.

How to track it: Yes or no. Keep the notes themselves on paper or in a notes app, and use your tracker only to record whether you did the exercise.

3. The pause before reacting

“Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things.”

Epictetus, Enchiridion 5 (trans. Elizabeth Carter)

Epictetus separates an event from the judgment you add to it. A colleague’s short reply is an event; “they don’t respect me” is a judgment. The Stoic habit is to notice that gap before acting on it. In the first section of the Enchiridion, Epictetus advises meeting a harsh impression with a reminder that it is only an impression, not necessarily what it seems to be.

This idea later became central to cognitive therapy. Albert Ellis, who developed rational emotive behavior therapy, and Aaron Beck, who developed cognitive therapy, both acknowledged a debt to the Stoics, according to a 2024 review in Discover Psychology. That shared history is interesting, but it doesn’t make a daily Stoic pause a substitute for therapy.

Small daily version: When you feel irritation rising, name the judgment in a few words before you reply: “I’m assuming they meant it rudely.” Then decide whether the reply still makes sense.

How to track it: A count-based habit. Log each time you catch yourself and pause. A target of one pause a day is enough; you are building the noticing, not grading your temper.

4. One idea a day

“after you have run over many thoughts, select one to be thoroughly digested that day.”

Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 2 (trans. Richard M. Gummere)

In his second letter to his friend Lucilius, Seneca warns against reading everything and absorbing nothing. Each day, he suggests, take in something that will help you face hardship, and choose one thought to think about properly. He then shares the thought he chose that day.

It is a useful corrective to collecting quotes. One line you return to during the day does more work than fifty you scroll past.

Small daily version: Read for ten minutes from a Stoic text, such as one of Seneca’s letters or a few sections of the Meditations. Copy one sentence onto a card or into your notes and come back to it once during the day.

How to track it: A time-based habit with a 10-minute target. Our guide on building a daily reading habit covers choosing a reading time and restarting after a break.

5. Count the days

“count the days on which you have not been angry. I used to be in passion every day; now every second day; then every third, then every fourth.”

Epictetus, Discourses 2.18 (trans. George Long)

Long before habit apps, Epictetus recommended keeping a count. To weaken a habit you don’t want, in this case anger, he advises you to stop feeding it and keep track of the days it doesn’t win. “But if you have intermitted thirty days,” he adds, “make a sacrifice to God.” By then, he explains, the habit has started to lose its grip.

Notice what he is counting. His progress is not an unbroken streak. It is the falling frequency of bad days: every day, then every second day, then every fourth. That is a forgiving and realistic way to read your own record.

Small daily version: Choose one reaction you want less of, such as snapping at family, complaining, or scrolling in bed. Each evening, note whether today was a day it didn’t take over.

How to track it: It is usually easier to record a positive action than an absence, so phrase it as one: “calm evening” or “phone out of the bedroom.” Then look at the monthly count, not only the current streak. A 30-day printable tracker matches Epictetus’ thirty-day horizon, and our guide to breaking bad habits explains how to replace a reaction rather than simply resist it.

6. Voluntary discomfort

“Set aside a certain number of days, during which you shall be content with the scantiest and cheapest fare, with coarse and rough dress, saying to yourself the while: ‘Is this the condition that I feared?’”

Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 18 (trans. Richard M. Gummere)

Seneca wrote this letter in December, while Rome celebrated the Saturnalia festival. His suggestion was to rehearse poverty on purpose, in comfortable times, so that losing comfort later would be less frightening. He is explicit that it should be a real test, not a wealthy person’s game of playing poor.

A modern habit can work on a much smaller scale. The aim is to show yourself that a little less comfort is manageable, so that convenience has less of a hold on you.

Small daily version: Once or twice a week, choose one minor inconvenience: walk instead of making a short drive, take the stairs, leave your phone in another room for an evening, or skip a purchase you would make out of habit. Keep it safe and modest. This is practice, not a test of endurance.

How to track it: A weekly target, such as two sessions per week, works better than a daily one. Flexibility across the week keeps the practice voluntary.

7. Memento mori: use the day

“Since it is possible that thou mayest depart from life this very moment, regulate every act and thought accordingly.”

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.11 (trans. George Long)

Reminders of mortality run through Stoic writing, and they are less gloomy than they sound. Seneca opens his first letter by urging Lucilius to “hold every hour in your grasp” (Moral Letters 1), because time, he says, is the only thing that truly belongs to us. The practical message is about priorities: don’t spend your best hours on things you would never choose if you stopped to think.

Small daily version: When you plan your day, ask one question: “What would I regret postponing again?” Do that task, call, or conversation first, before the day fills up.

How to track it: Yes or no. If thinking about mortality feels heavy right now, keep only the planning question. It carries the practical part of the exercise.

8. The evening review

“What bad habit of yours have you cured to-day? what vice have you checked? in what respect are you better?”

Seneca, On Anger 3.36 (trans. Aubrey Stewart)

Seneca credits these questions to the philosopher Sextius, who asked them of himself every night. He then describes his own version: once the lamp is taken away and his wife, “who knows my habit,” has stopped talking, “I pass the whole day in review before myself, and repeat all that I have said and done.” He looks at his mistakes candidly, then lets himself off with a note on what to do differently next time.

The habit-tracking version of this question is gentler than “did I fail today?” It looks for one small improvement and one thing to adjust, which makes it easier to repeat every night.

Small daily version: Five minutes before bed, answer three questions in writing or in your head. What went well? Where did I fall short of how I want to act? What will I do differently tomorrow? Keep each answer to one sentence.

How to track it: A daily yes-or-no habit with a reminder about 30 minutes before your usual bedtime. It works well as the last step of an evening routine, and it closes the loop with the morning preview: the morning sets an intention, and the evening checks it.

A simple Stoic daily routine

If you want the practices to work together, here is one way to fit four of them into an ordinary working day. It takes about 20 minutes in total, half of it reading.

How to start without turning Stoicism into a chore

A list of eight practices can easily become eight new obligations, which is not very Stoic. These steps keep it manageable:

  1. Choose one practice for one problem. Mornings feel rushed? Try the preview. Replaying conversations at night? Try the evening review. Overreacting at work? Try the pause.
  2. Attach it to an existing cue. “After I pour my coffee” or “after I brush my teeth” is easier to remember than “sometime in the morning.” Our guide to starting a daily habit walks through choosing a cue.
  3. Define the smallest version that counts. One sentence of evening review counts. Two minutes of morning preview counts.
  4. Record the action, not the mood. Track whether you did the practice, not whether you felt calm afterwards. How you feel depends partly on things outside your control, as Epictetus would point out.
  5. Review after a few weeks. Like Epictetus with his count, look at how often the practice happened, not whether the streak is perfect. If you keep missing it, change the cue or shrink the practice.

If you are wondering how long it takes for a practice to feel automatic, see what studies show about habit formation. There is no universal number of days.
