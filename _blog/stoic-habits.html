This article turns eight of those exercises into habits you can start this week. Each one comes with the original line, a little context, a small version you can do today (most take five minutes or less), and a way to keep track. You don’t need to read the philosophy first or adopt all eight. One practice done on most days beats a full Stoic regimen abandoned by Friday.

What Stoic practice is, and what it isn’t

Stoicism began in Athens around 300 BCE, when Zeno of Citium started teaching at the Stoa Poikile, the “painted porch” that gave the school its name. The writers most people read today are three Romans: Seneca (c. 4 BCE to 65 CE), a statesman and playwright; Epictetus (c. 50 to 135 CE), a teacher who was born into slavery; and Marcus Aurelius (121 to 180 CE), the emperor whose private notebook we now call the Meditations.

They thought about character the way we think about fitness: something you build by repetition. Epictetus put it in terms any habit tracker would recognize:

“Every habit and faculty is maintained and increased by the corresponding actions: the habit of walking by walking, the habit of running by running.”

Epictetus, Discourses 2.18 (trans. George Long)

One warning before you start. In everyday English, “stoic” means gritting your teeth and hiding your feelings. That isn’t the philosophy, and it may not serve you well: in a 2022 study of 1,307 university students in New Zealand, Norway, and the United States, that popular “stoic ideology” went along with lower well-being. The ancient Stoics wanted to examine the judgments behind an emotion, not to bottle it up.

1. The morning preview

“Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial.”

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.1 (trans. George Long)

Marcus Aurelius ruled an empire, and he still started some days expecting to meet people who would be nosy, rude, or ungrateful. So he said it to himself first. Then he reminded himself that people act like this out of ignorance of what is good, and that he was made to work with them, not against them. Modern writers often call this kind of rehearsal premeditatio malorum: thinking through troubles in advance.

The point isn’t pessimism. It is deciding how you want to act while you are calm, instead of improvising in the middle of a tense meeting.

Marcus had harder mornings, too. Book 5 opens with him arguing with himself in bed. He tells himself he is “rising to the work of a human being,” then asks, “Or have I been made for this, to lie in the bed-clothes and keep myself warm?” And he answers the way most of us would: “But this is more pleasant.” Even emperors wanted five more minutes.

Try it: Before you open messages, take two minutes. Name one situation today that could test you, such as a tense meeting, a long commute, or a tired evening at home, and decide on one response you want to practice.

Track it: a daily yes-or-no habit, with a reminder when you usually wake up. It slots easily into a morning routine.

2. The control check

“Some things are in our control and others not.”

Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 (trans. Elizabeth Carter)

These are the opening words of the Enchiridion, a pocket handbook of Epictetus’ teaching compiled by his student Arrian. Epictetus had more reason than most to think about control: he began life as a slave. His list of what is truly ours is short. Our opinions, intentions, desires, and aversions are up to us. Our bodies, property, reputation, and status never fully are.

Applied to a worry, the idea splits one heavy problem into two lighter ones. Take a job interview. Preparing three examples, sleeping well, and leaving early belong to you. Who else applied and what mood the interviewer is in do not.

Try it: Once a day, write down whatever is taking up the most space in your head. Underline the part you control, write one next step for it, and let the rest wait.

Track it: a daily yes-or-no habit. Keep the notes on paper or in your notes app; your tracker only needs to know you did it.

3. The pause before reacting

“Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things.”

Epictetus, Enchiridion 5 (trans. Elizabeth Carter)

A colleague replies to your message with one word. That is the event. “They’re annoyed with me” is the story you add, and it’s the story that spoils your afternoon. Epictetus’ advice is to notice the gap between the two before you act, and to treat a harsh first impression as an impression, not a verdict.

If that sounds like modern psychology, it partly is. Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck, the founders of two of the main forms of cognitive therapy, both credited the Stoics, as a 2024 review in Discover Psychology describes.

Try it: When you feel irritation rising, name the story in a few words before you reply: “I’m assuming they meant it rudely.” Then decide whether the reply still makes sense.

Track it: a count. One pause a day is a good target. You are practicing the noticing, not grading your temper.

4. One idea a day

“after you have run over many thoughts, select one to be thoroughly digested that day.”

Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 2 (trans. Richard M. Gummere)

Seneca was writing to his friend Lucilius, worried that reading too many authors would leave him scattered. His advice: read fewer books more carefully, and take one idea with you each day. Then, true to his word, he shares the thought he picked that day.

It is a good antidote to scrolling past quote accounts. One line you come back to at lunch does more than fifty you have forgotten by dinner.

Try it: Read for ten minutes from a Stoic text, such as one of Seneca’s letters or a few sections of the Meditations. Copy one sentence onto a card or into your notes, and look at it once more before evening.

Track it: minutes, with a 10-minute daily target. Our guide to building a reading habit covers choosing a time and restarting after a break.

5. Count the days

“count the days on which you have not been angry. I used to be in passion every day; now every second day; then every third, then every fourth.”

Epictetus, Discourses 2.18 (trans. George Long)

Nearly two thousand years before habit apps, this reads a lot like instructions for one. To weaken a habit you don’t want, in his case anger, Epictetus says to stop feeding it and count the days it doesn’t win. “But if you have intermitted thirty days,” he adds, “make a sacrifice to God.” By then, he explains, the habit has started to lose its grip.

Notice how he describes progress: anger shows up less and less often, then disappears for longer stretches, up to thirty days in a row. Our modern adaptation is to look at your monthly count alongside your current streak. That is an editorial suggestion, not Epictetus’ rule, but it lets you see progress even in a month with a missed day.

Try it: Pick one reaction you want less of, such as snapping at family, complaining, or scrolling in bed, and a replacement action. Each evening, note whether you practiced that replacement.

Track it: define an observable action, such as “paused before replying when irritated” or “put my phone outside the bedroom before bed.” Record whether you did it, then review the monthly count alongside your current streak. A 30-day printable tracker gives you a month of entries, and our guide to breaking bad habits shows how to replace a reaction instead of simply resisting it.

6. Voluntary discomfort

“Set aside a certain number of days, during which you shall be content with the scantiest and cheapest fare, with coarse and rough dress, saying to yourself the while: ‘Is this the condition that I feared?’”

Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 18 (trans. Richard M. Gummere)

Here is the twist: Seneca was one of the richest men in Rome. He wrote this letter in December, during the Saturnalia festival, while the city celebrated. His advice was to rehearse poverty on purpose while times are good, so that losing comfort later would feel less frightening. He insists it be a real test, not the playful imitation of poverty that wealthy Romans sometimes staged for fun.

You don’t need hard bread and a rough cloak. A small, chosen inconvenience is enough to remind you that comfort is pleasant, not essential.

Try it: Once or twice a week, pick one minor inconvenience: walk instead of making a short drive, take the stairs, leave your phone in another room for an evening, or skip a purchase you would make out of habit. Keep it safe and modest. This is practice, not an endurance test.

Track it: a weekly target, such as twice a week. The flexibility keeps it voluntary.

7. Memento mori: use the day

“Since it is possible that thou mayest depart from life this very moment, regulate every act and thought accordingly.”

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.11 (trans. George Long)

Stoic writing is full of reminders that life is short, and they are less gloomy than they sound. Seneca opens his very first letter by telling Lucilius to “hold every hour in your grasp,” because time, he says, is the only thing that truly belongs to us. The message is about priorities: don’t hand your best hours to things you would never choose if you stopped to think.

Try it: When you plan your day, ask one question: “What would I regret postponing again?” Do that task, call, or conversation first, before the day fills up.

Track it: yes or no. If thinking about mortality feels heavy right now, skip the philosophy and keep just the question.

8. The evening review

“What bad habit of yours have you cured to-day? what vice have you checked? in what respect are you better?”

Seneca, On Anger 3.36 (trans. Aubrey Stewart)

Seneca borrowed these questions from the philosopher Sextius, who asked them of himself every night. Then he describes his own version, with a lovely domestic detail: once the lamp is taken away and his wife, “who knows my habit,” has stopped talking, “I pass the whole day in review before myself, and repeat all that I have said and done.” He is honest about his mistakes, then lets himself off with a note for next time.

That last part matters. A review that only asks “Where did I fail?” is hard to face every night. One that also notices what went well is easier to come back to. Write the answers down and you have a simple Stoic journal; the Meditations themselves began as Marcus Aurelius’ private notes to himself.

Try it: Five minutes before bed, answer three questions, on paper or in your head. What went well? Where did I fall short of how I want to act? What will I do differently tomorrow? One sentence each.

Track it: a daily yes-or-no habit with a reminder about 30 minutes before bed. It makes a natural last step for an evening routine, and it closes the loop with the morning preview: the morning sets an intention, and the evening checks it.

A simple Stoic daily routine

Here is one way to fit four of the practices into an ordinary working day. It takes about 20 minutes in total, and half of that is reading.

  • After your first coffee (2 minutes): morning preview. One likely difficulty, one chosen response.
  • During the day (a few seconds): the pause, before any irritated reply.
  • Commute or lunch break (10 minutes): one idea. Read a short passage and keep one line.
  • Before bed (5 minutes): evening review. Three questions, one sentence each.

Treat it as a menu, not a program. Start with one practice, usually the morning preview or the evening review, and add another only when the first feels automatic.

How to start without turning Stoicism into a chore

Eight practices can easily become eight new obligations, which is not very Stoic. These steps keep it light:

  1. Choose one practice for one problem. Mornings feel rushed? Try the preview. Replaying conversations at night? Try the evening review. Overreacting at work? Try the pause.
  2. Attach it to something you already do. “After I pour my coffee” is easier to remember than “sometime in the morning.” Our guide to starting a daily habit walks through choosing a cue.
  3. Define the smallest version that counts. One sentence of evening review counts. Two minutes of morning preview counts.
  4. Record the action, not the mood. Track whether you did the practice, not whether you felt calm afterwards. How you feel depends partly on things outside your control, as Epictetus would be quick to point out.
  5. Review after a few weeks. Look at how often the practice happened over the month, alongside your current streak. If you keep missing it, change the cue or shrink the practice.

Wondering how long it takes for a practice to feel automatic? See what studies show about habit formation. There is no universal number of days.
